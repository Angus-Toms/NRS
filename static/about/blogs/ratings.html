An athlete's PTD rating is a single number per discipline that tries to summarise how fast they are, relative to everyone else who has ever raced. Higher is better. There is no fixed maximum or minimum, ratings can in principle be negative, and elite athletes (including Junior and U23) start at 1500 across the overall, swim, bike, run, and transition categories. From there, every race they line up in nudges those numbers up or down based on how they actually went.

The mechanic is straightforward. Before the race, we compare every athlete's pre-race ratings against everyone else's to produce a predicted outcome. A large rating gap implies a large expected time gap: we expect a 2000-rated athlete to comfortably beat a 1000-rated athlete, while a 1700 should only narrowly edge a 1600. After the race, we compare what happened to what we expected:

$$ \Delta \text{Rating} \;\propto\; \text{actual performance} - \text{expected performance} $$

Beat expectations and you gain points. Fall short and you lose them. Discipline ratings update independently, so an athlete who blew the swim but ran fantastically can lose swim points and gain run points in the same race.

That single design choice does most of the work. Because everything is measured against expectation, the rewards line up with what we intuitively recognise as an impressive performance:

  • Winning matters, but how you win matters more. A 30 second win and a 3 minute win in the same field are very different statements, and the rating update reflects that. The bigger the margin over the field's expected performance, the bigger the gain.
  • Strong fields are where ratings are won. Beating, or even hanging with, a deep field of high-rated athletes is the fastest way to climb. A narrow win over a strong opponent can outweigh a runaway over a weak one.
  • Easy wins are not free points. Similarly, a high-rated athlete who only just scrapes past a weaker field has, by definition, underperformed against expectation. They can lose rating from a win. There is no fixed-points trophy at the bottom of the table to scoop up.
  • Every position is competitive. Rating change is driven by how the day went, not by finishing position. An athlete in 50th who sprints away from the run group around them is still going to gain run and overall rating, even if their overall placing is not going to move. This is especially valuable for developing athletes who race competitively without often finishing in points-paying positions.

This is also why ratings move gently for established athletes. The system learns what to expect from someone by watching them race a lot, and once it has a confident estimate, it does not panic on a single odd result. New athletes (with very little history to anchor them) move around quickly at first, and updates settle once they have ten or so races on the board.

What is wrong with the existing systems?

The two ranking systems most athletes and fans encounter, the World Triathlon Ranking and the PTO World Ranking, do not work this way. Both have real, structural problems, and most of them are versions of the same underlying issue: they are points tables that try to bolt adjustments on, rather than measuring performance against the field directly.

  1. Field strength is barely a factor. The WT system is a points table: every event is assigned a tier (WTCS, World Cup, Continental Cup, and so on), each tier has a fixed winner's score (1000, 500, 250, and so on), and points decrease by a fixed 7.5% per finishing position. Winning a World Cup is worth 500 points whether the field were World Champions and Olympic medalists or a handful of athletes who turned up because the race was nearby. PTO tries to address this with a Strength of Field score, but it does so by averaging the pre-race ranking points of the top 5 starters. A simple average is a basic approxmation of the entire field, tells us nothing about the individual disciplines, and still only accounts for a fraction of their overall points system.
  2. Time gaps do not matter. In the WT system, finishing position is all that counts (once you sneak inside the 8% cutoff). A winner who leads gun to tape and wins by 5 minutes earns exactly the same as a winner who scrapes home by half a second. This fails to credit an athlete for how decisively they won, or how close they came to winning.
  3. The result is bad incentives. Put 1. and 2. together and you get a system that quietly rewards athletes for picking soft races and punishes them for stepping up. A high-rated athlete travelling around to weaker races can rack up points faster than an equally fast athlete who races the deepest fields they can find. It also takes the urgency out of large parts of a race: an athlete who exits the swim well off the pack or finds themselves alone on the run knows their position is effectively settled, and with it their points, so there is little to gain from emptying the tank. The whole structure encourages athletes to farm wins rather than chase competition.
  4. Not everyone scores. Under the WT system, athletes must finish within 8% of the winner's time to score anything at all, and the 7.5% per-position drop means points trail off rapidly. Outside the top 30, athletes score virtually nothing, regardless of the size and quality of field, or how close they came to the higher positions. This is particularly rough on developing and junior athletes, who may spend several seasons racing competitively without ever cracking the points-paying positions: they have very little in official rankings to show for it and no good way to assess performance and track improvement.
  5. Hard cliffs in time. Both systems use rolling windows that expire on a date. In WT, results count at full value for a year, drop to one third for the next year, then vanish. In PTO, only the top 4 races within the last year count. An athlete's ranking can move significantly with no new races, simply because an old result aged across a boundary. We take a different approach. A PTD rating is a measure of an athlete's long-term performance, built from every race they have ever done, and nothing about the maths discards old results. The question of "are they still racing?" is handled separately by a simple active flag that switches off after 18 months without a race, so retired or long-absent athletes keep their historical rating but drop out of current leaderboards.

What you get on PTD

Most of the consequences are already implicit in the design above, but it is worth being concrete about what you can do with the system that you cannot do with either of the official rankings:

  1. Compare athletes who have never raced each other. Because every rating has been calibrated against everyone an athlete has raced, two athletes from different regions sit on the same scale, even if their calendars have never overlapped. That makes cross-region comparisons meaningful in a way that fixed-points tables fundamentally cannot manage.
  2. See where strength comes from. WT and PTO each give a single overall number. PTD gives five: overall, swim, bike, run, and transition. An athlete's profile makes it obvious whether their overall result is built on swim, on run, or on transitions, and that breakdown is much more useful for coaches and developing athletes than a single ranking position.
  3. Read the field of any historical race. Because every race has a standard attached, you can immediately see how deep a given start list was, and how it compared to others in the same gender and course bucket.

Known limitations

No rating system is perfect, and ours is no exception. A few things we are aware of:

  1. Convergence takes time. Because new athletes start at 1500 and updates are deliberately gentle, it can take several races before a newcomer's rating settles near their true level. In the meantime their rating tends to undershoot if they are genuinely strong, or sit higher than warranted if they are not. This is the price of stable ratings for established athletes, and we think it is the right trade, but it is worth knowing about when looking at first-year pros or athletes returning from a long break.
  2. Ratings are not directly comparable across disciplines. Rating updates are driven by time gaps, and time gaps look very different from one discipline to another. The clearest example is the bike on short course, where drafting compresses the field into a single pack and the front-to-back spread is often a handful of seconds. That tight spread feeds through into compressed bike ratings, so short-course bike numbers tend to look low relative to swim and run numbers for the same athletes. The ordering and gaps within a discipline is meaningful, but a 1700 swim and a 1700 bike are not claims of equivalent strength.
  3. We depend on good results data. Missing splits, incorrectly recorded overall times, and obscure races that never made it into the source databases all create gaps that propagate into the ratings. We do a lot of automated and manual correction work to clean this up, but anomalies do slip through.
